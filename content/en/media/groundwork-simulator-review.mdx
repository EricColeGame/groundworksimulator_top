## Every Bucket Changes the Landscape

The headline mechanic is persistent terrain deformation. Trenches you cut stay cut. Piles you build stay built. Slopes you steepen become tomorrow's traction problem, because the ground you leave behind is the ground you drive on next.

In practice, that turns site planning into a real decision rather than decoration. A convenient shortcut ramp today can become an impassable mud slope after a few dozen loads, and fixing it means parking the excavator and doing groundwork instead of digging for pay dirt.

### Hydraulic feedback and dig feel

The excavation model simulates hydraulic resistance: the bucket loads up, the machine strains, and you adjust your angle and available power to keep cutting. That's the difference between a sim and a clicking exercise, and it's the feature most likely to determine whether you bounce off in twenty minutes or lose a weekend.

| Mechanic | What it asks of you | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Terrain deformation | Route planning, slope management | Your own work creates future obstacles |
| Hydraulic resistance | Bucket angle and power control | Skill ceiling beyond "hold the button" |
| Traction and mud | Route selection under load | Hauling becomes a puzzle, not a drive |
| Water routing | Pump and hose placement | The wash plant stops without supply |

## From Dirt to Gold: The Prospecting Loop

The progression spine is a genuine chain rather than a single activity. You explore for gold-rich ground, drill and test deposits, pick a digging spot, then build out a wash plant. Material runs through sluice boxes, and concentrate finishes on a shaking table.

That's four distinct jobs — prospecting, excavation, washing, and processing — and each one feeds the next. It also means a badly placed wash plant costs you real time, since every bucket has to travel from the cut to the water.

This is where the sim differentiates itself from pure earthmoving games. You aren't scored on cubic yards moved; you're scored on recovery. That said, with one map in the Early Access build, the exploration phase has a defined ceiling right now. The developers have stated that additional maps and vehicles are the core of the roadmap, and that's the content most likely to change how the loop feels at hour thirty.

## Co-op, Water, and the Hauling Grind

Online co-op splits the work naturally: one player runs the excavator, another handles transport, and material keeps moving between the dig face and the wash plant. It's the most sensible way to play, because solo you're constantly switching machines instead of specializing.

| Role | Best for | Solo equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Excavator operator | Players who love dig feel and precision | You, half the time |
| Haul truck driver | Players who enjoy terrain and load management | You, the other half |
| Wash plant operator | Players who like system tuning and water routing | Squeezed in between loads |

Water is its own sub-system. Pumps and hoses have to be positioned and routed, and as the site sprawls, your plumbing has to keep up. It's unglamorous, but it's the kind of logistical friction that makes a worksite feel owned rather than rented.

Transport adds a second layer: loading machinery onto a trailer, hauling it by truck, and navigating tight turns and slopes with a heavy load. It's the weakest link for solo players and the best argument for bringing a friend.
