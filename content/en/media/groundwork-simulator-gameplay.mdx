## Groundwork Simulator Gameplay Basics: What You Actually Do

The pitch is simple: heavy machinery, a landscape you can reshape, and gold buried somewhere under it. The reality is three systems running at once. You move earth, you chase gold, and you keep the logistics of a working site from collapsing under their own weight. Ignore any one of those and progress stalls.

New players should start with the story mode. It doubles as a guided tutorial that walks you through the core mechanics before you are turned loose on the map. The Early Access build ships with one playable map, which is enough to learn the terrain and enough to make you want more.

Here is how the main activities fit together:

| Activity | What you use | What it gets you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Excavating | Excavator bucket and hydraulics | Trenches, ramps, and cut hillsides |
| Prospecting | Drill and test holes | Confirmation of gold-rich ground |
| Processing | Wash plant, sluice boxes, shaking table | Recovered gold from raw material |
| Water supply | Pumps and hoses | A wash plant that keeps running |
| Hauling | Truck and equipment trailer | Machines moved to a new worksite |
| Site upkeep | Grading and re-routing | Traction, access, and safer loads |

The genius of the design is that these are not separate minigames bolted together. The ramp you cut so your truck can climb is the same ramp your excavator has to work from. The pile you build to reach a high cut becomes the wall that blocks your next haul route.

## Excavation Physics and Terrain That Fights Back

Digging is the heart of Groundwork Simulator gameplay, and it is not a point-and-click affair. The excavation engine simulates resistance, so your bucket bites differently depending on angle, depth, and how much material is already packed inside it. Hydraulic feedback means you feel the machine strain when you ask for more than it can lift.

That has a practical consequence: technique beats brute force. Approach at a shallow angle to break the surface, then flatten out to fill the bucket. Lift slowly when the bucket is heavy. Reposition rather than forcing a bad cut from an awkward stance.

The terrain is an opponent in its own right. Mud, slopes, and rough ground turn every route into a decision, and a heavy trailer makes those decisions unforgiving.

| Terrain problem | What goes wrong | Sensible response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mud | Traction disappears mid-pull | Take a firmer line or improve the surface first |
| Steep slope | Load shifts, wheels spin | Cut a gentler ramp before hauling |
| Rough ground | Bucket skips, cuts come out ragged | Grade the working face and haul road |
| Heavy trailer | Momentum beats steering | Slow down and plan turns early |

Community reports from the launch window echo what the store page promises: the soil and water simulation is CPU-hungry, so stutter usually comes from physics load rather than your graphics card.

## The Gold Loop: Drill, Dig, Wash, Repeat

Gold is not lying on the surface waiting for you. Finding it is a process, and the process is where the simulation gets its hooks in. You explore for ground that looks promising, drill and test to confirm it, then commit machines and water to the spot.

Once material is moving, the washing chain takes over. Pay dirt goes through the wash plant, the sluice boxes strip out the concentrate, and the shaking table finishes the job. Each stage has a bottleneck, and the bottleneck moves as your operation grows.

| Step | Equipment | Goal | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Scout | Terrain reading and observation | Shortlist likely ground | Digging on a hunch |
| 2. Test | Drill | Confirm the deposit | Skipping the test hole |
| 3. Excavate | Excavator | Feed the plant steadily | Over-digging and stockpiling dirt |
| 4. Wash | Wash plant and sluice boxes | Capture concentrate | Running without steady water |
| 5. Finish | Shaking table | Recover the gold | Letting concentrate pile up unprocessed |

The lesson most players learn the hard way is that your wash plant sets your pace, not your excavator. Digging faster than you can process just builds piles you have to move twice.

## Solo vs. Co-op, Water, and Moving Heavy Iron

Online co-op is the feature that changes the shape of a session. Working alone, you are constantly switching roles — dig, drive, adjust water, dig again. With a partner, one player can stay in the excavator while the other shuttles material or manages the pump and hose network. The site stops waiting on you.

| Job | Solo reality | Co-op reality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digging | Frequent trips out of the cab | Continuous bucket time |
| Hauling | Digging stops when you drive | Material keeps moving |
| Water | Set up, then run back to check flow | One player keeps the plant fed |
| Expansion | Everything sequenced | Tasks run in parallel |

Water logistics deserve their own warning. Pumps and hoses have to be positioned and routed across the site, and as the worksite spreads out, yesterday's tidy setup becomes today's tangle. Plan your hose runs before you need them.

Transport is the other logistics puzzle. Loading machinery onto a trailer, then hauling it by truck through tight turns and slopes, is genuinely tense with a heavy load behind you. Sometimes the smartest move is improving the ground under your wheels before you get back to digging.

## Practical Tips for a Productive First Worksite

A few habits separate a smooth operation from an afternoon of digging yourself into a corner:

- **Build access before output.** Cut your ramp and haul road first; every later job gets easier.
- **Test before you commit.** A drill hole costs minutes. Relocating a wash plant costs an hour.
- **Match digging to processing.** Let the sluice boxes set your tempo.
- **Keep the water close.** Short hose runs are easier to maintain as the site expands.
- **Grade as you go.** A tidy surface preserves traction and saves tires-and-temper later.
- **Use co-op for throughput, not chaos.** Assign roles before the session starts.

The broader lesson about Groundwork Simulator gameplay is that it rewards planning over reflexes. Players who treat the site like a small business — access roads, steady feed, maintained water — spend more time recovering gold and less time recovering stuck trucks.

Check the table above. The minimum calls for a GTX 1070 and 16 GB of RAM, with 24 GB recommended, because soil and water physics load the CPU heavily. Steam Deck compatibility is currently listed as unknown.

Not with the story mode, which functions as a guided tutorial for the core mechanics. The difficulty curve comes from site logistics rather than controls — knowing where to dig, how to route water, and when to improve the ground before hauling.
